Scientists Are Empiricists

Empiricists base their conclusions on systematic observations — evidence from the senses, or from instruments that extend the senses. Not on intuition, experience, or authority alone.

The Theory-Data Cycle

Four Terms to Know

Theory

A set of statements that describes general principles about how variables relate to one another.

Hypothesis

A prediction about the outcome of your research, based on theory.

Data

A set of observations. Do they support the hypothesis and strengthen the theory — or fail to, leading to a revised theory or an improved design?

Preregistered

A study where the researcher states publicly, before collecting any data, what the outcome is expected to be.

Worked Example: Harlow’s Monkeys

The Cupboard Theory vs. the Contact Comfort Theory

  • Theory: infants attach to their mothers because mothers provide food (the “cupboard” theory) — versus attachment coming from comfort and contact itself.

  • Hypothesis: if contact comfort matters more than food, infant monkeys will prefer a soft, cloth “mother” over a wire “mother” that provides milk.

  • Data: the monkeys overwhelmingly preferred the soft cloth mother, even when the wire mother was the one with food — support for the contact comfort theory.

Weight of Evidence

  • Replication: a study whose results can be reproduced when it’s repeated.

  • Weight of evidence: a conclusion drawn from reviewing the whole literature — the proportion of studies consistent with a theory — not just one finding.

Studies Don’t Prove Theories

Language matters

Instead of “prove,” scientists say a study’s findings support or are consistent with a theory. A single finding can’t prove — or disprove — a theory on its own.

Good theories are falsifiable

A theory must lead to hypotheses that, when tested, could actually fail to support it. “I can fly (as long as no one’s watching)” isn’t a theory — there’s no way to test it.

Scientists Work in a Community

Science is self-correcting: a process where researchers make their work available for peer review, replication, and critique — with the goal of catching and fixing errors, not just publishing and moving on.

Scientists Make Their Work Public

  • Journals: peer-reviewed periodicals, written by qualified contributors, audience is mostly scientists and students.

  • From journal to journalism: findings then get picked up (and sometimes distorted) by the popular press.

  • Who do you think the audience is for a scientific journal — and does that change how a finding should be reported to the public?

Case Study: The Mozart Effect

  • What got reported: listening to Mozart raises intelligence — parents played classical music to infants, even to fetuses.

  • What the original study actually found: a temporary, short-lived effect on spatial-temporal reasoning — in adults, not children.

Same pattern as the blueberry headline

A real finding, in a real journal — stretched past what the data supported once it hit the popular press.
