Reading and Writing an APA-Style Paper

Reading and Writing an APA-Style Paper

Chapter 1

Dave Brocker Lecturer Farmingdale State College

Why This Matters

A Skill You’ll Use All Semester

Reading an APA-style paper can seem daunting — some articles run a dozen pages, and the Results section can send the statistically faint of heart running for the hills!

The fix isn’t reading faster. It’s reading the paper in the order it was built to be read, section by section.

The Six Sections of an APA Paper

Abstract

One paragraph, the whole study

Introduction / Background

Why this study? What’s already known?

Method

Who, what, and how

Results

What did the data show?

Discussion

What does it mean?

References

Where the claims come from

Abstract

One Paragraph, the Whole Study

The abstract is a 150–250 word summary that previews every other section: the question, the method, the key result, and why it matters.

TipRead the abstract first, always

It tells you whether the rest of the paper is worth your time — and what to expect from each section that follows.

Introduction / Background

Why This Study? What’s Already Known?

The introduction builds the case for the study, moving from broad context to the specific question:

  • What do we already know, based on prior research?
  • What’s the gap, disagreement, or open question?
  • What does this study specifically test, and what’s the hypothesis?

Method

Who, What, and How

The Method section is the recipe — detailed enough that another researcher could run the same study again.

  • Participants: who was studied, and how were they recruited?
  • Materials/Measures: what instruments, surveys, or apparatus were used?
  • Procedure: what did participants actually do, in order?

Results

What Did the Data Show?

Results reports what was found, without yet interpreting what it means.

  • Descriptive statistics (means, standard deviations)
  • The statistical test(s) used, and whether the result was significant
  • Tables and figures that summarize the pattern in the data
ImportantResults describes; it doesn’t explain

Save the “why” and “so what” for the Discussion section — mixing the two is a common early-writer mistake.

Discussion

What Does It Mean?

The discussion interprets the results in light of the original question:

  • Did the data support the hypothesis?
  • How does this fit with (or challenge) prior research from the Introduction?
  • What are the limitations, and what should future research look at?

References

Where the Claims Come From

Every claim in the Introduction and Discussion should trace back to a source listed here, in APA citation format. This is also your best tool for finding more papers.

Pause and Discuss

Finding Related Work

Pick one article from your literature search. If you clicked “Cited By” on it, what showed up — newer studies that built on it, or studies that argued against it?

Check Your Understanding

You only have time to skim one paper before lab. Which section do you read first, and why? The Abstract — it’s built to answer exactly that question in under a minute, and tells you whether the full paper is worth a closer read.
A paper’s Results section reports a result, but never says what it means for the field. Is that a problem? No — that’s expected. Interpretation belongs in the Discussion section, not Results.

Key Takeaways

Reading and Writing an APA-Style Paper

  • APA papers follow a fixed structure for a reason: it lets you read strategically instead of front-to-back
  • Abstract → Introduction → Method → Results → Discussion → References, each with its own job
  • You’ll draft each of these sections yourself this semester, one lab at a time
